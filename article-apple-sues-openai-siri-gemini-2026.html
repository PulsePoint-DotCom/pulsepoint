Apple Sues OpenAI for Trade Secret Theft — and Drops ChatGPT for Google Gemini

By PulsePoint Staff · 2026-07-27 · 5 min read

In one of the most dramatic twists in the ongoing AI arms race, Apple has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI for theft of trade secrets — and simultaneously confirmed that its new Siri, launching this autumn, will run on Google Gemini instead of ChatGPT. The move marks a complete and official end to the Apple-OpenAI partnership that began just two years ago with massive fanfare.

What Apple Is Alleging

According to the lawsuit filed on July 10, 2026, Apple claims OpenAI misappropriated proprietary hardware technology developed during the Siri-ChatGPT integration partnership that started in 2024. The trigger: OpenAI's $6.4 billion acquisition of IO Products — the hardware company behind Jony Ive's AI device project — which Apple says directly encroached on technology shared in confidence during the partnership period.

When Apple opened Siri to ChatGPT in 2024, it was hailed as a landmark moment for consumer AI — putting GPT-powered intelligence into the hands of roughly 1.5 billion iPhone users worldwide. OpenAI gained something equally valuable: a foothold inside the world's most lucrative consumer hardware ecosystem. Now, Apple is alleging that OpenAI used what it learned from that partnership to build competing hardware products.

Siri's Gemini Switch: What Changes for Users

Starting this fall, when you ask Siri a complex question, it will route to Google Gemini instead of ChatGPT. That's a meaningful shift. Google Gemini is already deeply embedded in Google's own ecosystem — powering AI Mode in Google Search and available through Gemini Advanced. For Google, Siri integration is another major distribution channel. For OpenAI, it's the loss of what was arguably its biggest consumer reach advantage.

Gemini's integration into Siri is expected to bring some tangible differences: tighter Google Workspace connectivity, potentially better real-time search grounding, and a unified AI assistant experience across Android and iOS devices. Whether Gemini can match or exceed ChatGPT's capabilities for the kinds of tasks Apple users rely on remains to be seen — benchmarks suggest Gemini 3.5 Pro already leads on science and research tasks, but ChatGPT still dominates creative and coding workflows.

Why the Timing Is Everything

The lawsuit wasn't filed at a random moment. OpenAI's S-1 registration was submitted on June 8, 2026, and the company is targeting an IPO valuation of $830 billion to $1 trillion. A trade secret lawsuit from the world's most valuable company — Apple sits at roughly $3 trillion — is exactly the kind of material litigation risk that requires mandatory disclosure in an S-1 filing.

Apple's lawsuit couldn't have better timing from a strategic standpoint: it's landing right when OpenAI needs its cleanest possible IPO narrative. The combination of the lawsuit and the Siri distribution loss is, by any measure, the most significant negative news OpenAI has faced since its S-1 filing. OpenAI declined to comment on pending litigation.

The End of the AI Partnership Era

This case is bigger than Apple vs. OpenAI. It's the clearest signal yet that the "cooperation phase" of Big Tech AI is officially over. In 2024, Apple integrated ChatGPT because Apple didn't have a competitive AI model and OpenAI had the best available technology. By 2026, that calculation has completely changed: Apple has years of Siri-user interaction data to train on, has built internal AI capabilities, and now has a stronger partnership with Google than it ever had with OpenAI.

The precedent this case sets will reshape every AI partnership going forward. The fundamental question — can hardware IP shared during an AI integration partnership be used by that partner to build competing products? — has never been tested at this scale. The outcome will determine how labs like Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta structure their hardware and integration deals.

For now, one thing is certain: the era of Big Tech AI partnerships built on mutual convenience is over. The competition phase has arrived, and it's playing out in courtrooms as much as it is in data centers.
